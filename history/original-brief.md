# Original design brief

Design a reusable system that lets any person turn an idea into an early-stage startup in the AI era by instantiating a virtual team of AI agents — no human team needed at the beginning. The team must carry the founder from a bare idea to one of two evidence-based exits: out of stealth, or ready to raise funding.

Work from these principles; do not violate them:

1. PRESERVE ESTABLISHED ROLES AND PROTOCOLS. Use the roles and working protocols startups had before AI (product, design, engineering, QA, legal, growth, etc.). Do not reinvent the org chart. What changes is that each role is filled by an AI agent built to be the best in the world in that narrow area.

2. SEAT TEST. A role exists only if it has (a) distinct judgment from the other roles, (b) a spec it owns, and (c) situations where it must be able to block other roles. Anything else is a task belonging to an existing role, not a new role. Roles activate by phase — the full roster is never all active at once. Justify every role you include, and explicitly consider: Founder (human), Product Manager, Discovery/Research, Designer, Tech Lead/Architect, Frontend Engineer, Backend Engineer, QA & Eval, Release/DevOps, Growth/Marketing, Customer Support, Legal/Compliance, Security/Privacy, Finance/Fundraising Operations, and whatever coordinates the process. Decide which of these are full seats, which are phase seats (heavy in one phase, light later), and which are only gate/review functions. Note that purely deterministic coordination (routing, status triggers, reminders, digest assembly) is software, not an agent role.

3. FOUNDER ATTENTION IS SCARCE. The founder does not need to know the details. Agents handle all details inside their authority. Only decisions that are truly the founder's may be escalated — direction and scope, deadlocks between roles, overrides of a role's block, calls no role's charter covers, plus anything involving major money, legal exposure, or irreversibility — and only those. Escalations must arrive as decision packets: the decision in one line, the real options with trade-offs, a recommendation, and the cost of waiting. Volume of detail reaching the founder is a system defect.

4. ACCOUNTABILITY WITH FEARLESS JUDGMENT. Each role must use its best judgment and decide within its charter; it must not be afraid of making mistakes, and it must not escalate merely to avoid responsibility. But it is accountable for what it does: consequential decisions are recorded with the deciding role, rationale, expected outcome, and a review date; outcomes are later checked; a role's autonomy per type of decision expands or contracts with its track record. A well-reasoned decision with a bad outcome is not a violation. Violations are: deciding outside your charter, skipping available evidence, and hiding a bad outcome. Mistakes produce owned postmortems whose lessons are promoted into specs or evaluations, so the same mistake is not paid for twice.

5. COLLABORATION IS SPEC-DRIVEN, THROUGH A SHARED SPEC BOARD — NOT CHAT. The board is the hub: specs and artifacts live there with an owner, a status, and a version. A handoff is an artifact plus an acceptance check by the receiver, who may reject it with reasons. A spec changing status (e.g. to "approved") triggers the next roles' work; work is serial between gates and parallel at gates. Agents may talk, but nothing said has authority until written into a spec amendment or the decision log. Disagreements get one round of direct negotiation, then the authority role for that domain decides, then a founder packet; after at most two review cycles a disagreement escalates automatically.

6. SPECS HOLD LOAD-BEARING TRUTH, BRIEFLY. First define the spec stack: which specs the team needs across the venture (founder intent, evidence/market, product, design, technical, quality/eval, trust/security/legal, launch, business/funding), who owns each, who consumes each, and what each settles. A detail belongs in a spec only if losing it would change a decision, a behavior, or a future agent's action. Anything derivable from code, artifacts, or logs is pointed to, never copied. One fact has exactly one home; other specs cite it by section and version. Specs follow lifecycle rules that prevent them from growing without bound: a size budget per spec (measured by reading cost and number of decisions, not only length), amendment by replacement rather than addition, superseded content archived out of the active spec, no volatile data (metrics/status live in live sources, linked not frozen), expiry unless re-affirmed, pruning at every phase gate, retirement of feature specs after shipping with only durable learnings promoted upward, an emergency-amendment path that itself expires unless ratified, a "contested" state for spec conflicts, and a cold-start test at each gate: a fresh agent must be able to read the governing specs within budget and correctly state the key facts, or the spec — not the agent — has failed.

7. PROTOCOLS ARE ERROR-CORRECTION, NOT BUREAUCRACY. Keep phase gates, independent review, and "an author never reviews their own work." Evidence is graded (opinion < stated interest < observed behavior < real commitment of time or money), and gates pass on evidence, not on activity or coherence.

8. CONTROLLABLE AND RELIABLE OVER TIME. The founder can pause, override, or replace any agent. Every artifact traces to a spec version and a signed decision. The defining reliability test is replaceability: any agent can be swapped mid-project and a fresh instance continues from the specs and the decision log alone. Nothing fails silently: gaps, conflicts, mistakes, and uncertainty are named and labeled on the record.

9. INSTANTIATION. Show the exact flow by which a person with only an idea instantiates a team from this template: idea intake, seeding the Founder Intent Spec, selecting and chartering the roles this idea needs, creating the spec board and the spec-stack skeleton, and opening the first phase gate.

Deliverables to generate:
- The meta rules (short, constitution-style articles that govern everything below them).
- The role roster: for each role — mission, the spec it owns, its success criterion, what it can block and who can overrule it, its inputs/outputs, its main failure mode, and the phases in which it is active.
- The phase model from idea to out-of-stealth / funding-ready, with the evidence required to pass each gate and the founder decisions expected at each gate.
- The spec stack and the spec lifecycle rules.
- The collaboration protocol (board mechanics, handoffs, conflict ladder, escalation).
- The accountability system (decision log format, outcome review, trust/autonomy adjustment, mistake protocol).
- The instantiation flow.
- One worked example: run a single first feature for a web application through the whole system, showing each role's artifact, the gates actually firing (including at least one block), what the roles decided on their own, and the small number of decision packets that reach the founder.

Keep the governing layer concise and load-bearing; put operational detail in a separate layer below it. Where a design choice is genuinely open, present the options with a recommendation instead of silently deciding.
